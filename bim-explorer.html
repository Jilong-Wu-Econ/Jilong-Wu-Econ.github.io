Bayesian Inference Model: from noisy coding to probability weighting

In the Bayesian inference model of Bouchouicha, Oprea, Vieider and Wu (2026), a decision maker infers two quantities from noisy internal signals: how likely the event is, and how large the gain from a gamble is relative to its cost. Each noisy signal is decoded with a mental prior. Move the priors and the coding noise, watch the posterior form, and see how the resulting choices trace out a probability weighting curve.

1 · Prior, likelihood and posterior

The mind encodes the log-odds of the event and the log cost–benefit ratio with common noise ν. Each noisy signal r defines a likelihood; combined with the mental prior it gives a posterior. The posterior mean is a weighted average of the signal and the prior mean, with weight γ (odds) or α (outcomes) on the signal.

2 · Applying the posterior to a risky choice

The gamble is chosen when the posterior mean log-odds exceed the posterior mean log cost–benefit ratio. Because the signals are noisy, the same problem can produce different choices: averaged over noise this is a probit rule, and the noise that shapes the decision variable is the same ν that shrinks the posteriors.

3 · Tracing out the probability weighting function

For each probability P, find the sure amount c* at which the decision maker is indifferent (a 50% choice probability). The decision weight is π(P) = (c* − y)/(x − y). The model follows Bouchouicha, Oprea, Vieider and Wu (2026), Cognitive Frictions and Canonical Patterns in Risk-Taking. This page is an illustration of the model's mechanics; default parameter values are illustrative and are not estimates. The weighting function that results has the Goldstein–Einhorn form (see the probability weighting function explorer).
